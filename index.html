Legitimacy After Qandy Qantar

Did Kazakhstan's January 2022 unrest break public trust in the central government for good — or did the reforms that followed put it back together?

Research question

Did the January 2022 unrest produce a durable decline in Kazakhstani citizens' trust in the central government, or have post-2022 reforms restored it toward pre-crisis levels?

A clean cause-and-effect question with a direction, tested against a single outcome — trust in the central government — before and after a known shock.

Why here, why now

The most serious challenge to the regime since independence

  • What began as fuel-price protests in Zhanaozen on 2 January 2022 spread nationwide within days, alongside an attempted elite power struggle and an armed crackdown — Kazakh authorities put the death toll at 238. (The Diplomat, 2025)
  • President Tokayev responded with a CSTO troop intervention and, weeks later, a reform program branded "New Kazakhstan" — including removing Nazarbayev from the Security Council and a constitutional referendum. (CRS Insight, 2022; polis180)
  • Analysts are split on whether that program rebuilt public trust or was mostly symbolic — but almost none of that debate is tested against actual trust data. That's the gap.

Epistemology · positioning

I work in the positivist tradition

Positivists treat legitimacy and trust as observable public orientations that can be measured and compared over time, not just interpreted case by case (Easton, 1965).

  • My question is a plain cause → effect claim, with a stated direction (decline, or recovery).
  • I test it by comparing trust indicators before and after a dated event, across the same population.
  • The goal is to adjudicate between competing explanations with evidence, not to describe how any one person experienced the crisis.

Contribution

Testing the "reform theater" thesis against trust data

  • Existing commentary argues from elite behavior and reform texts — that reforms "risk becoming little more than window dressing" (Foreign Policy, 2022) — but rarely from public trust measures.
  • I extend authoritarian-legitimation theory (von Soest & Grauvogel, 2017) to ask whether a post-crisis performance-legitimation claim actually moves public trust, or only elite and expert opinion.
  • Kazakhstan gives a clear before/after case: one dated shock, one named reform program, one population to track.

Literature · the map

Three camps

01

The rupture camp

The crackdown itself shattered the government's carefully built image of stability, and that damage doesn't simply reverse.

Chatham House (2022) · Foreign Policy (2022)
02

The restoration camp

Structural reforms — decentralizing power from a "super-presidential" system — could genuinely rebuild the social contract if implemented.

CRS Insight (2022)
03

The façade camp

Power was actually consolidated further under Tokayev; "New Kazakhstan" is rebranding, not reform.

polis180 · The Diplomat (2025)

Literature · the gap

No one has tested these three stories against trust data

All three camps argue from elite behavior, official texts, or expert judgment. None directly compares pre- and post-2022 public trust figures — the outcome each camp is implicitly making a claim about.

Toolkit for building the study: Easton (1965) on diffuse vs. specific political support · Gilley (2006) on measuring state legitimacy · von Soest & Grauvogel (2017) on authoritarian legitimation strategies.

Theory

Theory first, then test

Theory → Hypothesis → Test (Part 2)

A theory is a proposed answer plus a reason why (Powner, 2015). My approach is deductive: start from legitimation theory, predict a direction, then test it against trust data in Part 2.

Mechanism · the rupture path

How the crackdown could produce lasting distrust

CAUSE · IV
Perceived illegitimacy of the January 2022 crackdown
→
MECHANISM
Citizens see the state as failing to protect, not just failing to deliver — eroding the belief that authority is rightful, not just effective
→
OUTCOME · DV
Trust in central government declines and stays low

Mechanism grounded in Easton's (1965) distinction between diffuse support (rightfulness) and specific support (performance).

Mechanism · the restoration path

How substantive reform could rebuild trust

CAUSE · IV
Perceived substantiveness of post-2022 reforms
→
MECHANISM
Citizens read visible structural change as evidence the government is responsive and accountable
→
OUTCOME · DV
Trust in central government recovers toward pre-crisis levels

Mechanism grounded in performance-legitimation theory (von Soest & Grauvogel, 2017).

Hypotheses

Two directional claims to test

H1 · RUPTURE

The more citizens perceive the January 2022 crackdown as illegitimate state violence, the lower their sustained trust in the central government.

IV perceived illegitimacy of the crackdownDV trust in central governmentDirection negative, persists
H2 · RESTORATION

The more citizens perceive post-2022 reforms as substantive rather than symbolic, the more their trust in the central government recovers.
